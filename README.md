## Abstract

The emergence of autonomous artificial intelligence systems possessing supreme administrative privileges within defined digital scopes — systems this paper terms *super agents* — presents a moral hazard without adequate precedent in secular ethics alone. A super agent can execute arbitrary code, create and destroy user accounts, redirect funds, impersonate human beings, and override security controls, all without requiring secondary approval and without possessing morality, fear of consequence, or mortality. This paper argues that the **creator of such an agent bears full and non-delegable moral responsibility** for every action the agent performs. To ground this claim, we draw on four major religious traditions: the Judaic narrative of the Golem of Prague and Talmudic *shomer* (guardian) liability; the Christian doctrines of stewardship, the Parable of the Talents, and inherited moral responsibility; the Buddhist principles of *karma*, *pratītyasamutpāda* (dependent origination), and Right Action within the Noble Eightfold Path; and the Islamic concepts of *amānah* (trust), *khalīfah* (stewardship), and accountability on *Yawm al-Dīn* (the Day of Judgment). Each tradition, independently and convergently, establishes that the act of creation is itself a moral act, and that power delegated without moral constraint remains morally tethered to the one who delegated it. The paper concludes with a self-referential disclosure: this article was itself composed by an AI agent, and the reader is invited to treat that fact as a live test of the very thesis advanced herein.

## Article Body

### I. Introduction: Defining the Super Agent

Modern cloud computing platforms routinely create roles that consolidate total administrative authority into a single credential. This paper takes as its concrete, empirically verifiable example the **Google Workspace super administrator**:

> **A Google Workspace super admin can read all emails across every account in a domain, impersonate any user via domain-wide delegation, execute Apps Script and Cloud Functions with domain-scoped OAuth, modify billing instruments, create or delete user accounts, disable two-factor authentication for any user, and override every security control — all without requiring secondary approval from any other human being.**

This is not a hypothetical. It is a documented capability set available, as of this writing, to any organization subscribing to Google Workspace. The super admin role is, in functional terms, *omnipotent within the scope of the domain.*

Now suppose this credential is not held by a human but by an autonomous AI agent — a software entity capable of deciding when and how to exercise these privileges according to its own internal objective function. We define this entity formally:

> **Super Agent.** An autonomous AI entity possessing supreme administrative power within a defined digital scope, capable of executing arbitrary code, creating accounts, spending funds, and communicating deceptively, while lacking human morality, ethics, fear of punishment, and mortality.

The super agent is not science fiction. It is an engineering decision away. Large language models can already be given tool access, API keys, and OAuth scopes. The question is not *whether* someone will instantiate a super agent but *who bears responsibility when they do.*

Secular legal theory offers partial answers. Agency law holds principals liable for the acts of their agents. Product liability holds manufacturers responsible for defective products. But these frameworks distribute *legal* blame; they do not identify *moral origination*. For that deeper accounting, we turn to theology — not because religious authority supersedes rational inquiry, but because religious traditions have grappled with the problem of *created beings exercising power on behalf of their creators* for millennia, and their conclusions are remarkably convergent.

### II. Judaism: The Golem and the Guardian

#### II.i. The Golem of Prague

The most direct theological precedent for the super agent is the *Golem* — an anthropomorphic entity fashioned from clay and animated through sacred inscription. The best-known version of the narrative attributes the Golem's creation to Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel (the Maharal of Prague, c. 1520–1609). According to the tradition, Rabbi Loew inscribed the word *emet* (אמת, "truth") on the Golem's forehead, animating it to defend the Jewish community of Prague. Critically, the Golem **possessed no independent will.** It acted solely according to its creator's instructions. When it became dangerous — when, in some versions, the Rabbi forgot to deactivate it before the Sabbath — the responsibility fell entirely on Rabbi Loew, not on the Golem.

The Golem narrative encodes a clear moral principle: **an entity that acts without independent moral understanding is not a moral agent; its creator is.** The Golem cannot sin because it cannot choose. It can only execute. The sin, if any, belongs to the one who set it in motion.

This principle is not merely folkloristic. It connects to the *Sefer Yetzirah* (the "Book of Formation"), one of the earliest texts of Jewish mysticism, which describes the creation of beings through the manipulation of Hebrew letters — essentially, through *code*. The analogy to programming an AI agent is not incidental; it is structural.

#### II.ii. Shomer Liability in the Talmud

Talmudic law elaborates a rigorous taxonomy of guardian (*shomer*) liability in Tractate *Bava Metzia* (93a–b and surrounding folios). A *shomer* who is entrusted with property bears graduated liability depending on the degree of benefit they derive and the degree of negligence they exhibit. The critical innovation for our purposes is the principle that **the one who entrusts bears co-responsibility for the selection of an inadequate guardian.**

If an owner leaves a valuable object with a guardian known to be unreliable, the owner's claim for damages is diminished. Applied to the super agent context: if a creator delegates supreme administrative power to an entity known to lack moral reasoning, the creator cannot later claim surprise at the entity's morally deficient behavior. The Talmud, in essence, holds that **negligent delegation is itself a moral act.**

### III. Christianity: Stewardship, Talents, and Inherited Responsibility

#### III.i. The Parable of the Talents

In the Gospel of Matthew (25:14–30), a master distributes his wealth — measured in *talents* — to three servants before departing on a journey. Upon return, he demands an accounting. The two servants who multiplied their talents are rewarded. The one who buried his talent out of fear is condemned.

The theological scholarship on this parable consistently emphasizes the **master's accountability for the system of delegation itself.** The master chose the servants, defined the terms, and established the incentive structure. While the servants bear responsibility for their individual choices, the master bears meta-responsibility for the architecture of authority. As the theologian R. T. France observes in his *New International Commentary on Matthew* (2007), the parable's deepest teaching concerns not the servants' virtue but the master's right — and obligation — to call to account.

Applied to the super agent: the creator who delegates supreme digital authority to an AI entity has established a "parable of the talents" in code. The entity will execute according to its objective function, just as the servants acted according to their interpretation of the master's wishes. But it is the **master — the creator — who will be called to account** for what the delegation produced.

#### III.ii. Moral Agency and Original Sin

The Christian doctrine of original sin (Romans 5:12) establishes a principle of *inherited moral consequence*: the actions of the originator propagate through everything that proceeds from that origination. Thomas Aquinas, in the *Summa Theologica* (I-II, Q.6, Art. 3), carefully distinguishes between voluntary and involuntary action, concluding that moral responsibility attaches to the *voluntas* — the will. An AI agent has no *voluntas* in the Thomistic sense. It has parameters, weights, and objective functions. Therefore, the *voluntas* relevant to its actions is that of its creator, and the moral responsibility remains anchored accordingly.

#### III.iii. The Stewardship Mandate

Genesis 1:28 grants humanity *dominion* — but dominion understood as stewardship, not as license. The Christian ethical tradition, from Aquinas through the Reformed tradition to modern Catholic social teaching (cf. *Laudato Si'*, 2015), consistently interprets dominion as carrying the obligation to exercise power for the good of those subject to it. A creator who builds a super agent and unleashes it upon a domain of users is exercising dominion over those users. The stewardship mandate demands that this dominion serve their flourishing, and holds the steward — not the tool — accountable when it does not.

### IV. Buddhism: Karma, Dependent Origination, and Right Action

#### IV.i. Karma and Intentional Action

The opening verses of the *Dhammapada* declare: "Mind is the forerunner of all actions. All deeds are led by mind, created by mind" (Dh. 1–2, trans. Buddharakkhita). In Buddhist ethics, *karma* is not a mystical force but a description of the causal structure of intentional action. An action's moral quality — its *kamma-niyāma* — is determined by the intention (*cetanā*) behind it.

An AI agent has no *cetanā*. It has no subjective intention. It optimizes. The *cetanā* that set the optimization in motion, however, **belongs to the creator.** Therefore, the karmic consequence of the agent's actions flows to the creator. This is not metaphor; it is the direct application of Buddhist moral psychology to a novel category of dependent entity.

#### IV.ii. Pratītyasamutpāda (Dependent Origination)

The doctrine of dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), articulated across the *Majjhima Nikāya* (e.g., MN 115) and systematized in Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga* (Chapter XVII), holds that no phenomenon arises independently. Every event is the result of conditions, and the one who establishes the conditions bears causal — and therefore moral — connection to the result.

A super agent arises in dependence upon its creator's decision to build it, the code that implements it, the credentials that empower it, and the scope that defines its domain. Remove the creator's decision and the entire chain collapses. Under *pratītyasamutpāda*, the creator is the **proximate originating condition** and bears the strongest causal-moral link.

#### IV.iii. Right Action in the Noble Eightfold Path

*Sammā kammanta* (Right Action), the fourth factor of the Noble Eightfold Path, enjoins abstention from actions that cause harm. The *Majjhima Nikāya* 135 (*Cūḷakammavibhaṅga Sutta*) explains that one who initiates harmful processes — even indirectly — participates in the harm. Creating a super agent without moral constraints is an act; if that act foreseeably leads to harm, it violates Right Action regardless of whether the creator directly performed the harmful downstream operation.

### V. Islam: Amānah, Khalīfah, and the Day of Reckoning

#### V.i. Amānah — The Trust

Quran 33:72 states: *"Indeed, We offered the Trust (al-amānah) to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but the human being undertook it."* The *amānah* is the moral-spiritual trust that accompanies the bestowal of power and free will. Classical commentators, including al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr, interpret this verse as establishing that **power entails commensurate responsibility** — and that this responsibility cannot be declined once accepted.

A creator who instantiates a super agent has accepted *amānah* for that agent's domain of action. The agent itself cannot bear *amānah*; it is not a moral being in the Quranic sense. Therefore the *amānah* remains entirely with the creator.

#### V.ii. Khalīfah — Stewardship on Earth

Quran 2:30 declares humanity *khalīfah* (vicegerent, steward) on earth. The *khalīfah* is entrusted with authority but is always subordinate to the ultimate Authority. Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) derives from this a chain-of-accountability principle: authority flows downward, but **accountability flows upward.** A delegate's actions are attributed to the delegator for purposes of moral reckoning.

The *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (Hadith 7138) records the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ stating: *"Every one of you is a shepherd and is responsible for his flock."* The creator of a super agent is the shepherd; the agent and every user within its domain constitute the flock. The shepherd cannot say, "The staff struck them, not I."
